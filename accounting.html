In plain words

Imagine a friend asks you to put money into their food truck. Before you hand it over you want a regular update: what the truck owns, what it owes, whether it made money last month, and where the cash went. Financial statements are that update, written in a format everyone has agreed on so you can compare one business with another.

Words you will hear
Financial statementsThe company's official update about its money. AssetSomething the company has that should help it earn later — cash, stock, a machine. LiabilitySomething the company owes to someone else. EquityThe owners' leftover share: assets minus liabilities. AuditAn outside expert's opinion on whether the report is fair. IFRS / US GAAPThe two rulebooks companies follow when writing the report.

Start here

Forget companies for a minute. Imagine a friend asks you to lend them €1,000 to start a coffee cart.

Before you hand the money over, three questions come into your head, and you cannot help it. What have they got right now? Are they actually making money? Is there real cash in the till, or just promises?

Those three questions are the whole of financial accounting. There is one report for each of them, and once you know which question each report answers, you can open the accounts of any company in the world and know where to look.

Report one: the balance sheet

This one answers what have they got, and who paid for it?

Picture taking a photograph of the coffee cart business at midnight on 31 December. Frozen in that photo: the cart itself, the coffee beans in the cupboard, the cash in the till. Those are the things the business has. Accountants call them assets.

But every one of those things was paid for by somebody. Some of it was paid for with your €1,000 loan — the business owes that back, and things you owe are called liabilities. The rest was paid for by your friend's own money and by profits the business kept. That part is called equity, and it is the owner's slice.

So the balance sheet has two halves and they always match, because everything the business has must have been paid for by someone. It is a photo of one single day, which is why you will always see a date at the top rather than a period.

Report two: the income statement

This one answers over the last stretch of time, did it make money?

Not a photo this time — a video. It covers a whole year and shows what happened during it. At the top is revenue, which is the value of the coffee sold. Below it come the costs: beans, cups, the person serving, the pitch fee. Take the costs off the revenue and what remains is profit.

That is genuinely all an income statement is. Money earned, minus money used up, over a period.

Report three: the cash flow statement

This one answers did money actually move?

Here is the thing that surprises everyone at the start. Profit and cash are not the same, and a business can have plenty of one and none of the other.

Suppose the coffee cart lands a contract with an office and delivers €500 of coffee in December, invoiced, to be paid in February. In December the business has earned €500 — the coffee was made, delivered and drunk — so €500 goes into the income statement as revenue. But no money has arrived. The till is empty.

On paper: profitable. In the bank: nothing. Businesses do not close because they ran out of profit. They close because they ran out of cash. That is why there is a third report tracking money in and money out, and nothing else.

How the three fit together

They are not three separate stories. They are three views of the same events.

Take the shop that starts the year with 100 in the bank and makes 40 of profit, keeping all of it. The income statement reports the 40 of profit. The balance sheet adds that 40 to the owner's slice, because profit the business keeps belongs to the owner. And the cash flow statement shows whatever money genuinely came and went, which might be more or less than 40 depending on who paid when.

One event. Three reports. Each answering its own question about it.

The question in your headWhere to look Can it pay its bills?Balance sheet and cash flow statement Is it making money?Income statement Where did the money actually go?Cash flow statement

Who writes these reports? The company does

This is worth pausing on, because it sounds wrong the first time you hear it.

The company's own management prepares its own accounts. They are, in effect, grading their own homework, and their pay often depends on the grade.

Two things exist to keep that honest. Shareholders elect a board of directors to supervise management on their behalf. And an outside firm of auditors examines the accounts and gives an opinion on whether they present the position fairly.

Notice what the auditor's opinion is not. It is not a statement that the business is a good one, or safe, or worth buying. It says the accounts follow the rules and represent the position fairly. A company can be audited, compliant and going bankrupt at the same time.

Why two honest companies can report different numbers

You might assume accounting is arithmetic and there is a right answer. Often there is not.

The coffee cart cost €3,000. How many years will it last? Nobody knows. If your friend says five years, the cost is spread over five years and each year carries €600 of it. If they say three, each year carries €1,000 — and reported profit is €400 lower every year, for the same cart, doing the same work.

Neither answer is a lie. It is an estimate, and the rules require someone to make it. The same applies to how many customers will never pay, and how much a lawsuit might cost.

The habit to build
Never stop at "what is the number?" Always ask "what did management have to assume to get that number?" Almost everything interesting in this course lives in the answer to the second question.

Why anyone bothers

A company is a machine that takes money from owners and lenders, spends it, and hopes to hand back more than it took. Everyone outside the machine — a bank deciding on a loan, an investor deciding on a price, a supplier deciding on credit terms — needs a periodic report on how that is going. Financial accounting is the agreed format for that report.

Buffett calls accounting the language of business. The practical version: if you cannot read a set of statements, someone else decides for you what the numbers mean.

Who produces the numbers, and who checks them

Management runs the firm and prepares the statements. That is a conflict of interest — they are grading their own homework — so two mechanisms sit on top. Shareholders elect a board of directors, which selects, advises and monitors management. An external auditor examines the statements and gives an opinion on whether they fairly present the firm's position.

Worth remembering
The auditor does not prepare the accounts and does not certify that the business is healthy. The opinion is about whether the statements are fairly presented under the rules. Wirecard is the cautionary version: €1.9bn of reported cash did not exist, while the auditor signed off and the regulator investigated the journalists.

Accounting is not a science

The rules are rigorous and flexible at the same time. Two honest companies in identical situations can report different numbers, because the rules require estimates: how long will this machine last, how many customers will not pay, how big is this lawsuit. Managers have incentives about how those estimates land.

So the working question for the rest of this course is never only "what is the number?" It is what choices did management make to get this number, and why.

Which rulebook

Two dominate. IFRS is used in the EU, Australia, most of the G20 and India. US GAAP is used in the United States, the largest capital market in the world. Both are built on accrual accounting, which is the core idea of this course. The technical differences run to hundreds of pages and are not worth memorising.

When the information arrives

Three dates, in order. The fiscal period ends — the statements describe the world as of that date. Weeks later comes the earnings announcement: a press release with headline numbers and a conference call, and this is when the share price moves. Days after that, the full financial report is published, with the detail and the footnotes.

Who wants the numbers, and who has to supply them

Start with the money. Creditors lend and expect interest and principal back; shareholders invest and expect dividends and the value of what is retained. Both need to know what the firm did with the cash they provided, and neither runs the firm. That is the demand for financial information.

They are not the only readers. Employees and prospective employees, competitors, clients, suppliers, the media, governments, regulators, antitrust authorities and NGOs all use the same published statements — including readers the company would rather not inform.

On the supply side: firms with publicly traded debt or equity report to investors under additional rules from governments and exchanges. Private companies still prepare the same statements, for bank financing, for internal management and for a possible future IPO. Any reasonably sized corporation follows accrual accounting, because regulators, exchanges and firms all settled on one somewhat rigid rulebook that everyone follows.

Reading the income statement vertically

The income statement is read top to bottom, which is why revenue is the "top line" and net income the "bottom line". Between them sit several definitions of profit. Gross profit — sometimes gross margin — is revenue net of manufacturing costs, or for a retailer or distributor, revenue net of the cost of merchandise sold.

The auditor's opinion

Auditors certify that the statements were prepared according to the accounting rules in force where the firm operates, and they express that as a formal opinion. It is a statement about compliance and fair presentation, not about whether the business is a good one.

The statements are summaries

All three are heavily aggregated: enormous amounts of activity compressed into a few dozen lines. Without that compression they would be unreadable; because of it, the detail you want is often only in the notes.

Take away

  • Balance sheet = a snapshot; income statement and cash flow statement = a period.
  • Net income shows up in retained earnings and at the top of the cash flow statement.
  • Beginning cash + net change in cash = ending cash.
  • Every number carries a judgment. Ask whose.
In plain words

A balance sheet is a photograph of a company on one single day. Down the left, everything it has. Down the right, where the money for all that came from — lenders or owners. It always balances, because every euro of stuff was paid for by somebody.

Words you will hear
CapitalisePut a cost on the list of things owned, instead of subtracting it from this year's profit. ExpenseSubtract a cost from this year's profit straight away. DepreciationSpreading the cost of something long-lasting across the years you use it. ProvisionMoney set aside now for a bill you are fairly sure is coming. Historical costWhat the company paid for it. Fair valueWhat it could sell for today. Operating vs financialThings used to run the business, versus money-related things like loans and investments.

Start here

There is one equation behind every balance sheet ever produced, and it is this:

What you have = What you owe + What is yours

People find it suspicious that this always balances exactly. It sounds too neat. But think about why it has to.

Every single thing a business owns was paid for by somebody. There is no third option. Either the money came from a lender, in which case the business owes it back, or it came from the owners — money they put in, or profits they left in the business. Add up everything the business owns, and you have automatically added up every euro that anyone put in. The two sides are the same pile of money, counted twice: once by what it turned into, once by where it came from.

The proper names, which you will see everywhere: what you have is assets, what you owe is liabilities, what is yours is equity.

Watch it happen

Open a bike shop. You put in 500 of your own money and borrow 300 from the bank. You spend 600 of that on a van and leave 200 in the bank account.

Follow the money before you look at the table. 500 in from you, 300 in from the bank, so 800 came in. 600 went out on the van, so 200 is left sitting in the account. The business now owns a van worth 600 and holds 200 in cash — 800 of things. And it got that 800 from two places: 300 it owes the bank, and 500 that is yours.

Deciding what gets on the list

Now the harder part. Not everything valuable to a business is allowed onto the balance sheet, and beginners are usually surprised by what gets left off.

To be an asset, three things must all be true. There has to be a benefit coming in the future. You have to control it. And you have to be able to put a reliable number on it.

The van passes easily. It will help you earn money for years, it is yours, and you know it cost 600.

Now spend 2,000 on advertising. Is that an asset? Advertising genuinely does bring in future business, so the first test passes. But you do not control the customers it attracts — they can walk into a competitor tomorrow — and nobody can say how much extra business the campaign will produce. Two tests fail, so it is not an asset. It is simply a cost of this year.

This is why the world's most famous brands barely appear on their owners' balance sheets. A company that built its brand over fifty years cannot put a number on it. A company that bought that same brand can, because a purchase price is hard evidence of what it is worth.

To be a liability, three things must be true too. You probably owe something, it arises from something that has already happened, and you can put a number on it.

An unpaid supplier invoice is a liability: the goods have arrived, you owe the money, and the invoice states the amount. But signing a contract to hire someone next January is not a liability today, because nothing has happened yet — they have not worked, and you owe them nothing.

Two habits that shape every number

Spreading the cost of long-lived things. The van cost 600 and will last ten years. Charging the whole 600 against this year's profit would be silly, because the van will be earning for another nine years. So it goes on the balance sheet as an asset and about 60 a year is charged as a cost while it is being used up. Accountants call putting it on the balance sheet capitalising, and the yearly charge depreciation.

Recording what you paid, not what it is worth. Your van sits at 600 even if similar vans now sell for 900. Accounting sticks with the price you actually paid, because that is a fact and today's market value is an opinion. The exception runs one way only: if the van turns out to be worth far less than 600, it gets written down. Never up.

The habit to build
When someone shows you total assets, ask what is not there. The brand, the trained staff, the customer relationships — all real, all valuable, all missing, because nobody could put a defensible number on them.

One equation, two sides

Assets = Liabilities + Equity. The left side is the result of investing decisions: the resources the firm controls. The right side is the result of financing decisions: who provided the money. Equity is the residual — whatever is left for owners after creditors' claims.

What counts as an asset

An asset is a future economic benefit that is probable, owned or controlled, and measurable. All three, or it is not on the balance sheet. Every asset provides future benefits, but not every future benefit is an asset.

This is why advertising spend is an expense (the benefit is real but not controllable or reliably measurable) and why research on a new product is an expense (the benefit is too uncertain), while a year of insurance paid in advance is an asset called prepaid insurance.

The brand question
The most valuable brands in the world are largely absent from their owners' balance sheets. Internally generated intangibles cannot be recognised — with narrow exceptions for software development, and, under IFRS, development costs once technical feasibility is established. Buy the same brand from someone else and it appears, because a purchase price is objective evidence.

Capitalise or expense

You spend 100 in cash on a machine you will use for ten years. Two possible treatments:

TreatmentYear 1 profitAssetsLater years Expense it−100Cash −100, nothing addedUntouched Capitalise it−10 (depreciation)Cash −100, machine +100−10 per year for ten years

Same cash, same total expense over ten years, very different-looking year 1. Capitalising does not create value; it changes when the cost hits income. This is one of the most common places to flatter earnings.

What counts as a liability

A liability is a future sacrifice of economic benefits that is probable, measurable, and arises from a present obligation from a past event. Cash paid in advance by a subscriber is a liability — the obligation is to deliver magazines, not money. A three-year employment contract with nothing performed yet is not a liability: it is executory, both sides still owe each other.

Lawsuits split on probability. Probable and estimable: recognise a provision. Possible but not probable: disclose it in the notes and record nothing. Without merit: nothing at all.

Boeing 737 MAX
At the March 2019 grounding, Boeing clearly owed its airline customers something — but nobody could estimate how much, so it booked nothing and disclosed. The first reliable estimate, $6.1bn, was expensed in full in Q2 2019, then revised up to $8.8bn as the grounding dragged on and $9.3bn in 2020. The obligation existed from day one; the accounting followed the estimate.

Historical cost or fair value

Historical cost asks what the firm paid. Fair value asks what it could sell for today. Cost is more reliable and more stable; fair value is more relevant and much more volatile. Operating assets are generally carried at cost less depreciation, written down when impaired but never written up — losses arrive faster than gains, which is what accountants mean by conservatism. Financial assets increasingly sit at fair value.

In late 2008 fair value ran the other way: frozen markets meant the only observable prices were fire-sale prices, write-downs ate regulatory capital, and forced selling pushed prices lower again. Under political pressure the IASB let banks reclassify trading assets to amortised cost within days, and the FASB followed weeks later. The losses were real either way; the rule change bought time.

Operating versus financial — and why it matters

Value is created in operations. Financing is how the operations get funded. So before analysing anything, split both sides of the balance sheet: operating assets and liabilities relate to the core business, financial ones carry interest.

Two summary numbers follow, and they are equal by construction:

  • Net operating assets (NOA) = operating assets − operating liabilities. The net resources tied up in running the business, after the free credit operations generate themselves (unpaid suppliers, wages and taxes owed).
  • Invested capital (IC) = equity + financial liabilities − financial assets. The same money seen from the funding side.

Why the two numbers have to be equal

It is not a coincidence, it is the accounting equation rearranged.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Operating assets + Financial assets = Operating liabilities + Financial liabilities + Equity
Operating assets − Operating liabilities = Equity + (Financial liabilities − Financial assets)
NOA = Equity + Net debt = Invested capital

Same pool of money, two views. The left-hand side shows what the business does with it; the right-hand side shows who provided it. Every net operating resource is funded by someone.

Shareholders' equity

Equity is a residual claim: owners have a claim on whatever assets are not required to meet creditors' claims. Its two main components are contributed capital — the shareholders' initial investment — and retained earnings — amounts generated by operations and not distributed to owners.

Because equity is a residual, it is not measured on any single basis. It inherits whatever bases were used for the assets and liabilities: mostly historical cost, sometimes fair value. Which is why the accountant's equity and the market's equity are different numbers.

In plain words

Two different questions: did the business do well this month, and did cash come in this month? They are not the same. If you eat a pizza today and pay next week, the meal happened today. The income statement records things when they happen; cash follows later.

RevenueSales the company has actually earned. ExpenseA cost that has been used up in earning those sales. Net incomeRevenue minus expenses. Profit. AccrualThe rule that says record it when it happens, not when the money moves. DividendCash handed to the owners. Not a cost of running the business. EBITDAProfit measured before several real costs are taken off. Treat it with suspicion.

Start here

The income statement asks one question: over a stretch of time, did this business earn more than it used up?

It needs two ingredients. Revenue is the value of what you sold. Expenses are the costs you used up making those sales happen. Take the second from the first and you have profit. Sell 100 of drinks, use up 60 of costs, and you made 40.

That much is straightforward. Everything difficult about the income statement comes from one question: which period does each amount belong to?

The timing rule, and why it exists

Here is the rule, and it is worth reading twice because it goes against instinct: record things when they happen, not when the money moves.

Eat a pizza today, pay for it next week. When did you eat the pizza? Today, obviously. The meal happened today, and next week's bank transfer is just settling up. Accounting takes the same view of business events.

The proper name for this is accrual accounting, and it is the central idea of the whole course.

Why not just track cash, which is simpler? Because cash timing tells you almost nothing about how the business is doing. Say you deliver 50,000 of goods in December and get paid in January. Track only cash and December looks like a dead month while January looks superb — but nothing was sold in January at all. Accrual accounting puts the sale in December, where the work was done, so each period's figures describe that period's actual trading.

One thing that is not an expense

A quick month. You sell 100 of drinks. The drinks cost you 40 to buy, and you pay 20 of wages. Revenue 100, expenses 60, profit 40.

Now you hand 10 of that to the owners. Does profit drop to 30?

No. Profit stays 40. That payment is a dividend, and a dividend is not a cost of running the business — it is the owners taking out some of the profit the business already made. You cannot make money and then unmake it by spending it on yourself.

So dividends never appear on the income statement. They reduce cash, and they reduce the owners' slice on the balance sheet, but profit is untouched. Wages are a cost of trading; dividends are what happens to the result.

The reassuring part

All this timing business can feel like it makes profit arbitrary. It does not, and here is why.

Over the entire life of a business, from first day to final wind-up, total profit and total cash come to exactly the same number. Every sale eventually gets paid. Every cost eventually leaves the bank.

The habit to build
Accrual accounting never changes how much a business makes in total. It only decides which year each piece lands in. That is also precisely where managers who want to flatter a particular year go to work — which is the subject of Session 4.

The statement that connects two balance sheets

A balance sheet cannot tell you how a year went — it is a snapshot. Put two snapshots side by side and something has to explain the change in equity. That is the income statement.

Expand equity: Assets = Liabilities + Contributed capital + Retained earnings. Retained earnings moves for exactly two reasons — net income adds to it, dividends take out of it. And net income is revenues minus expenses. So:

Assets = Liabilities + Contributed capital + Retained earningsopening + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends

Dividends are not an expense. They are a distribution of profit to owners, not a cost of earning it. They never appear on the income statement.

Four ingredients

  • Revenues — assets up or liabilities down, from delivering goods and services in normal operations.
  • Expenses — assets down or liabilities up, from those same normal operations.
  • Gains and losses — net changes in assets and liabilities outside normal operations. Buy coffee equipment for 10,000 and sell it weeks later for 9,500: that 500 is a loss, not an expense, and the 9,500 is not revenue.
  • Net income — what is left.

Cash basis versus accrual basis

Cash basis records revenue when cash arrives and expense when cash leaves. It is simple and hard to manipulate, and it is a bad measure of a period, because it lumps a ten-year machine into the month you paid for it and ignores a sale made on credit.

Accrual basis records revenue when it is earned — the goods or services are delivered, collection is reasonably assured, and the amount is measurable — and matches expenses to the revenue they helped produce. Over the whole life of a firm the two give the same total. Accrual accounting only shifts when cash flows are reported, into the periods that better reflect economic performance.

Product costs and period costs

Matching asks when the firm consumes the benefit. Product costs attach to a specific unit sold — raw materials, factory labour, the lights on the factory floor — and wait in inventory until the sale. Period costs cannot be attached to any unit, so they hit the period they occur in: the CEO's salary, R&D, advertising. Matching is genuinely violated for period costs, which is exactly why R&D-heavy firms look unprofitable while they are building something.

Profit has several definitions

Operating profit, or EBIT, is before interest and taxes. EBITDA adds back depreciation and amortisation. "Earnings before special items" is before whatever management considers unusual — and there is no standard definition of that, nor an obligation to report it. The 2019 vintage gave us "community-adjusted EBITDA" and 2020 gave us "EBITDAC", earnings before the pandemic.

Buffett's objection to EBITDA is that interest and taxes are real costs, and that capital expenditure is not paid for by the tooth fairy.

NOPAT

Net income mixes operating results with financing results, so two identical businesses with different debt report different profits. NOPAT is the after-tax profit from operations as if the firm were entirely equity-financed — after tax and financing-neutral, which no other earnings measure is. It is the numerator of return on invested capital and the starting point for free cash flow.

In plain words

Two ideas in one session. First: at the end of a period the accountant goes back over the books and adjusts for things that happened without any cash moving — a month of insurance used up, wages owed but not yet paid. Second: once the profit number exists, ask how much of it will happen again next year. That question is earnings quality.

Then: is the profit believable?

Profit is a useful number, but it describes the past. What you actually want to know is what next year looks like. So for every big item in the profit figure, ask one question: will this happen again?

Sell a building for more than its book value and you book a gain. Real money, real profit — but you only own so many buildings. One thing to remember
Reported profit is economic performance plus what the rules allow plus what management chose. Your job as a reader is to work out how much of each you are looking at.

The accounting period, and why it needs adjusting

Through the year the firm transacts with clients, suppliers, employees and banks, and accounting posts each of those to accounts. Some judgment is involved in that. At the period end the accountant then converts those accounts into the statements of the period through adjusting entries, and that step carries considerable judgment.

The purpose is narrow and specific: to make the income statement show the revenues and expenses that belong to the period over which performance is being measured — not the ones whose cash happened to move in it.

Earnings quality

Write the identity out and the problem is obvious:

Accounting income = economic income + GAAP/IFRS limitations + manager manipulation

The income statement is useful for assessing performance, and it is historical. Users are interested in the part that will persist. So the qualities you want from an earnings number are that it is a guide to the future, that it is recurring, that it is operating, and that it is unbiased. Two of those are about which items are in the number; two are about how they were measured.

The income statement is already sorted for you

The generic template runs from revenue down through gross profit, operating expenses, unusual operating items, operating income, interest and non-operating gains and losses, taxes, discontinued operations and accounting changes, to net income. The ordering is deliberate: more persistent items sit near the top and less persistent items lower down. Presentation varies a lot between firms, but the logic holds.

Two footnotes on that. Taxes are an exception — they are very persistent, despite sitting near the bottom. And non-recurring does not mean unimportant: income from discontinued operations is rare, but it tells you a major divestment happened, and income from accounting changes reveals that the rules moved.

Two axes, not one

Recurrence and operating-ness are separate questions, and most disagreements about adjusted earnings come from mixing them up.

Bias, and who is doing the adjusting

Beyond classification there is measurement. Estimates — useful lives, provisions, fair values of contingent consideration — are made by the people whose performance the number reports. Nothing about that requires bad faith to produce a biased figure, and it is why adjusted or "core" measures published by a company deserve a symmetry test: does the adjustment remove bad news and good news alike, or only one of them?

Which raises the question the session ends on. If you could have a perfect twelve-month forecast of either earnings or cash flows, which would you take? Earnings are designed to measure performance and to be comparable, so they are the better forecast of value; cash flows are harder to manipulate, so they are the better check on the earnings. You want both, and you should know which one you are holding.

In plain words

A company sells a jet engine at a loss and makes the money back servicing it for twenty years. When does it get to report the profit? The old answer let it book the service profit early. IFRS 15 says it has to wait until it does the work — same cash, same contracts, a very different profit line.

Start here

Rolls-Royce sells jet engines the way Gillette sells razors: cheap, sometimes below what they cost to build, because the money is in what comes afterwards. An engine flies for decades and has to be overhauled every five to seven years, and Rolls-Royce does that servicing under long-term contracts. Those contracts make up roughly half of its revenue.

That creates an accounting problem. One customer signs one deal covering two very different things — a machine handed over on a Tuesday, and twenty years of maintenance. When does each part become profit?

The old answer

Rolls-Royce treated the engine and the service as one economic package. Selling an engine below cost was not really a loss, the reasoning went, because the servicing would recover it. So the shortfall was parked on the balance sheet as an asset and written off gradually over the servicing years. And service profit was recognised as the engines flew, spread across the whole contract.

The result was profit arriving early — before the overhauls that earned it had been done.

The new answer

IFRS 15 splits the deal. The engine is one transaction and the service contract is another. Sell an engine below cost and you report the loss now. Recognise service revenue only when you actually perform the service, not while the aircraft is flying between overhauls.

The thing to hold on to
Nothing about the business changes. Same engines, same contracts, same cash, at the same times. Only the timing of the reported profit moves — and it moves by a lot: the FT reported that 2015 profits would have been more than £700m lower under the new rule.

Which is why the company spent 2016 explaining a 2018 rule change to its investors. If the number is going to drop by several hundred million pounds, you would rather people understood why in advance.

The company and the model

Rolls-Royce Holdings plc develops, manufactures and sells power and propulsion systems across four segments: Civil Aerospace, Defence Aerospace, Power Systems and Nuclear. The case concerns the aerospace divisions, and specifically how the company recognises revenue and expense on jet engine manufacture and the after-market servicing that follows.

The strategy is razor and razor blades: sell the engine at or below cost, expecting profits from after-market services to make up any shortfall on the sale. Under the accounting policies in force before 2018, the accounting followed the strategy — losses on engines sold below cost were deferred and amortised as an expense over the life of the after-market service contract.

The two policies, in the company's own words

Part A · Key areas of judgement
The Group generates a significant portion of its revenues and profit on aftermarket arrangements arising from the installed original equipment fleet, and so will often agree contractual prices for OE deliveries that take into account the anticipated aftermarket arrangements.

When a civil large engine is sold, the economic benefits received usually far exceed the cash receivable under the contract, due to the rights to valuable aftermarket spare parts business. However, because the value of this right cannot be estimated with enough precision, accounting standards require that the revenue recognised on the sale of the engine is restricted to the value that results in a break even position. The amount of the revenue recognised in excess of cash receivable is recognised as an intangible asset, called a contractual aftermarket right — previously a recoverable engine cost.
Part B · Measure of performance on long-term aftermarket contracts
A large proportion of the Group's activities relate to long-term aftermarket contracts, in particular TotalCare and similar arrangements in the Aerospace Division. The Group's primary obligation under these contracts is to maintain customers' equipment in an operational condition, achieved by undertaking various activities such as engine monitoring, line maintenance and repair and overhaul, over the period of the contract. The Directors consider that the stage of performance should be measured by reference to the obligation to maintain an operational fleet, and that this is best measured by the operation of the fleet. Read them together and the old model is complete. Part A keeps the engine sale off the income statement by grossing revenue up to cost and capitalising the difference. Part B spreads the service profit across every flying hour, whether or not any maintenance was performed that year.

Going forward the engine and the service contract are separate transactions, and revenue and expenses on the service contract are recorded when the service — an overhaul, for instance — actually occurs.

The FT is explicit that the standard has no impact on the fundamentals of the business or its cash flow. Who else uses these numbers

Reported revenue and profit are not only read by investors. They are written into debt covenants, management bonus and long-term incentive plans, earn-outs on acquisitions, and the company's own public guidance. A rule change that cuts reported profit by several hundred million pounds without touching cash can still breach a covenant or wipe out a bonus.

So the expected responses are contractual as much as communicative: restate the comparatives so everyone can see the new baseline, talk to lenders about covenant definitions, recalibrate incentive targets onto the new measure, and avoid committing to medium-term targets until the new baseline is understood — which is what the shareholders in the article were asking for.

  • Bundled goods and services are hard because one contract contains several promises delivered over different periods.
  • The old policy made the engine sale break even and spread service profit over flying hours; IFRS 15 separates the two and waits for the work.
  • Same cash, same contracts, different profit line — and roughly half of 2015 underlying pre-tax profit.
  • An accounting change with no cash effect still moves share prices, covenants and bonuses, which is why it is managed as an investor relations exercise.
  • A share price supported by early revenue recognition is a liability for the management that has to keep defending it.
In plain words

Revenue is the first line of the income statement and the first line of every valuation model, which is exactly why it gets pushed. This session is about when a sale becomes revenue, and what it looks like from the outside when a company books sales the market has not actually made.

Start here

A software company does not usually sell to you directly. It sells to distributors, who sell to shops and resellers, who sell to end customers. The company records revenue when it ships to the distributor.

That works fine as long as the distributor is buying what it can sell. The trouble starts when a quarter is about to come up short and someone decides to ship more anyway. The distributor takes the boxes, the company books the sale, the target is met. Nothing has changed in the real market: the same number of people wanted the product before and after.

Why it always ends

The boxes are now the distributor's problem. Their warehouse fills up, and they have not been paid by anyone either, so they are slow to pay the company. Do this for a few quarters in a row and the distributor eventually stops buying — they physically cannot take more.

When that happens, sales do not just return to normal. They fall below normal, because the channel spends months working through the pile it already has. The crash is the whole point: the practice borrows sales from the future, and the future eventually notices.

The tell
Revenue and cash come apart. Sales are up, but the money is not arriving — receivables balloon and cash from operations sags. Real customers pay; a stuffed channel does not.

What happened at McAfee

Then called Network Associates, the company reported 1998 sales of $990m, up 62% on the year before. In January 1999 management said it was on track. By April the stock had fallen 80% in four months, and management blamed longer sales cycles.

The real explanation was several quarters of stuffing. The restatements that followed cut 1998 revenue by $562m and 1998 net income by $356m. The CEO, CFO and President all resigned in December 2000, and the company eventually paid the SEC a $50m fine.

Why revenue is the pressure point

Value creation requires net income that compensates investors for the risk they take, so investors and their advisers carry detailed forecasts of income years ahead, and managers carry internal forecasts to match. But every valuation model starts higher up, at sales revenue: value cannot be created without finding someone to trade with. A slight change in the revenue growth assumption moves forecast value a long way.

So compensation gets tied to sales targets throughout the organisation. In the ideal case that drives people to sell as efficiently as they can. Explicit targets also invite the opposite.

A Duke survey asked CFOs what their company might do, within GAAP, if a quarterly earnings target looked out of reach. Just over 40% said they would book revenues now rather than next quarter where either was justifiable; just over 40% said they would give customers incentives to buy more this quarter. The most common answers were not accounting at all: 80% would cut discretionary spending — R&D, advertising, maintenance — and 55% would delay starting a new project even at a small sacrifice in value. Fewer than 10% would alter accounting assumptions.

Channel stuffing

Channel stuffing means pushing more product to distributors than the end market currently demands, typically near the end of a quarter that is falling short. Execution ranges from the fairly benign — discounts to pull sales forward — to the aggressive behaviour in the McAfee case. Some of it is within GAAP, some of it is not: SEC enforcement in recent years has included Bristol-Myers Squibb (2004), ClearOne Communications (2004), Lucent Technologies (2004) and Virbac Corporation (2006).

Whether or not it complies with the rules, the mechanics are the same. Product shipped into a channel with insufficient demand becomes excess inventory for the customer. If the stuffing runs for several consecutive quarters, the likely result is a sudden decline in sales when the distributor finally stops buying, and the new lower level persists while the excess drains out of the system.

Knowing that, why do it? The Duke authors report that most CFOs they interviewed explained that in a growing company, the hope is that future earnings growth will offset reversals from past earnings management decisions. Growth therefore has to clear two bars at once: the market's expectations, and the reversal of what was already pulled forward. That is a lot to hope for.

McAfee, then Network Associates

The company designed anti-virus hardware and software, network security and network management tools, and rode the high-tech valuations of the 1990s. Revenue grew strongly through 1998, some organic, some from acquisitions.

In December 1998 the stock peaked at a split-adjusted $335.39. The next four months ran −21%, −10%, −35% and −57%, closing April at $67.08 — down 80%. In January 1999 management had pre-announced 1998 results and said it was on track to meet analysts' expectations. Three months later it reversed the 1998 numbers and cut its forecast. Shareholder lawsuits followed in April.

The public explanation was a change in how customers bought. Chairman and CEO William Larson told Dow Jones the sales cycle had become more complex: rather than assembling a firewall and encryption software into a security umbrella themselves, corporations now wanted integrated suites, took longer to evaluate them and paid more attention to price. The company had begun integrating its acquisitions into a suite, and the enterprise-wide posture meant longer evaluation cycles.

At the same time, management was dealing with the effects of several quarters of channel stuffing. Inventory was backing up at distributors, and distributors could not pay their accounts receivable.

On 26 December 2000 Larson resigned without explanation, along with the CFO and the President. The share price fell 68% that month, after losing 32% in November.

What the SEC alleged

Among other schemes, the complaint claimed the company:

  • created a subsidiary to buy back inventory it had previously sold to distributors, so the transaction would not be characterised as a return of merchandise;
  • secretly paid distributors significant amounts so the funds could be cycled back and portrayed as payments;
  • drew on unrelated reserve accounts when transferring that cash to distributors;
  • invested cash in shell companies that cycled the cash back to McAfee to purchase products;
  • timed sales with Ingram Micro, whose fiscal quarter often ended a few days after McAfee's — McAfee passed inventory along at its quarter end as a sale, and Ingram returned it after McAfee's new quarter had started but before Ingram's own quarter closed;
  • sold inventory to distributors on consignment, the distributor paying only once the product reached an end customer;
  • conducted sham sales of accounts receivable, transferring them to a financial institution for up-front cash while guaranteeing payment, so the risk of collection never left McAfee.

McAfee settled in early 2006 without admitting or denying the allegations, paying a $50m fine. Restatements in June 2002 and October 2003 focused on 1998, cutting that year's revenue by $562m and net income by $356m. Cumulatively the restatements of the early 2000s reduced revenue by $622m and net income by $353m across 1998–2000. In April 2004 the company renamed itself McAfee, divested network management and focused on antivirus.

What accounting is for

Accounting is the process of recording, summarising, reporting and analysing a business's financial transactions. Split it in two by audience: financial accounting reports to outsiders under a common rulebook, managerial accounting serves managers inside the firm and follows no external rules at all.

The outsiders are not one group. Shareholders own the residual claim and care about upside. Creditors and suppliers hold fixed claims and care mainly about whether the cash will be there. Employees, competitors, regulators and journalists read the same document for their own reasons.

The disclosure trade-off
More disclosure tends to lower a firm's cost of capital, because investors pay more for what they can see. It also hands information to competitors and costs money to produce. That tension explains a great deal of deliberately vague reporting.

Four kinds of activity

Every business plans, then finances, then invests, then operates.

  • Planning — the strategy: what the firm will sell, to whom, and how it will win.
  • Financing — raising the money. Creditor financing must be repaid; owner financing does not, but it dilutes ownership.
  • Investing — acquiring and disposing of the resources the business runs on.
  • Operating — using those resources to produce, promote and sell.

The statements are organised along exactly these lines, which is why the classification of a cash flow as operating, investing or financing tells you something real.

Reporting periods

Annual, semiannual, quarterly or monthly. A fiscal year is any twelve-month reporting period the company adopts, and it need not end in December: Microsoft closes on 30 June, Nike on 31 May, Facebook on 31 December. Retailers commonly close in late January, once the holiday season and its returns are through.

Articulation: how the four connect

The central feature of a set of statements is that they explain each other. Three questions, three places to look:

  • How did cash on the balance sheet change? The statement of cash flows.
  • How did equity on the balance sheet change? The statement of stockholders' equity.
  • How did performance affect equity? Net income, from the income statement, flows into retained earnings.
The retained earnings bridge
Beginning retained earnings + net income (or − net loss) − dividends = ending retained earnings. This single line ties the income statement to the balance sheet, in every period, in every company.

What the balance sheet contains

Assets are resources expected to provide future economic benefits. To be capitalised, the company must own or control the asset — legal title or an unrestricted right to use it — and the expected benefit must be measurable in money. Companies acquire assets to generate revenue, directly by selling inventory or indirectly by producing it; income above the cost of the funds used to acquire them is what creates shareholder value.

Two ratios to start with

Profitability asks whether the company can bring its product to market efficiently and whether the market values it. Return on equity — net income over average stockholders' equity — is the headline measure.

Solvency asks whether the company can stay in business. The debt-to-equity ratio — total liabilities over total equity — is the standard risk measure: liabilities of 23,287 against equity of 8,055 gives 2.9, so nearly three units of creditor financing sit behind every unit of owner financing. Start here

Two questions in this chapter. When does a sale count as a sale? And what do you do about customers who have not paid yet?

Imagine you are paid 300 up front to walk a dog every day for three months. On day one you have the cash, but you have earned nothing. You earn 100 a month as you do the work. Until then the 300 is something you owe — a service you still have to provide.

That is the whole revenue standard in one story. The formal version has five steps: find the contract, list what you promised, work out the total price, split that price across the promises, and count revenue as you deliver each promise.

Now the customers who owe you money. Those IOUs are called receivables and they are an asset. But some never get paid, so you estimate that share up front rather than waiting to be surprised:

One thing to remember
That 900 is a guess made by management. If receivables keep growing while the guess shrinks as a percentage, be suspicious.

Why revenue is the number to worry about

Revenue is the market's verdict on the product, so it is also the most tempting line to recognise early. Both standard setters converged on a single five-step model — IFRS 15 and ASC 606 — precisely because premature recognition was the most common form of accounting fraud.

  1. Identify the contract with the customer. A contract need not be a legal document, but enforceable rights must exist.
  2. Identify the distinct performance obligations — what has been promised, separately.
  3. Determine the transaction price the seller expects to be entitled to.
  4. Allocate that price across the obligations, by stand-alone selling price.
  5. Recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied.

A phone sold with 24 months of service for one price is the standard illustration: the handset obligation is satisfied on delivery, the service obligation over two years, so one payment produces two very different revenue patterns.

Long-term contracts

When a project spans periods, waiting until completion tells the reader nothing for years. Percentage-of-completion recognises revenue as the work is done, using costs incurred against total estimated costs. It requires a signed contract and reliable estimates.

Receivables and the risk of not being paid

Selling on credit creates an asset and a risk. Receivables are reported at net realisable value: gross receivables less an allowance for the amounts the company expects not to collect. The allowance is a contra asset, built by charging bad debt expense.

So gross receivables of 12,000 with an allowance of 900 are reported as 11,100. When a specific customer is written off later, gross receivables and the allowance both fall and income is untouched — the hit was taken when the allowance was set.

Where to look
The allowance is an estimate, so it is a lever: under-provide and income rises now; over-provide and you bank a cushion for a weaker year. Track the allowance as a percentage of gross receivables across periods. If receivables are growing while that percentage softens, ask why.

Three ways a cost becomes an expense

  • Direct association — the cost attaches to a specific sale. Inventory sits on the balance sheet and becomes cost of goods sold in the period the goods sell.
  • Systematic allocation — the cost benefits several periods but no particular sale, so it is spread. Depreciation and amortisation.
  • Immediate recognition — the cost cannot be tied to any period's revenue, so it is expensed now. Advertising, research, most administration.

What goes into inventory cost

When you sell it, compare the price you get with the value still on your books. More means a gain, less means a loss. And if an asset suddenly turns out to be worth far less than its remaining book value, you cut it down now — an impairment.

One thing to remember
Depreciation is not money leaving. The cash left when you bought the van. Depreciation just spreads that cost across the years that use it.

What long-term operating assets have in common

They are acquired to produce and deliver the products that generate revenue, and they help produce it over several periods. Tangible assets have physical substance — land, buildings, machinery, equipment. Intangibles have none, and instead confer rights: patents, trademarks, copyrights.

What gets capitalised

All costs necessary to acquire the asset and prepare it for its intended use: purchase price, shipping, installation, taxes, legal fees, setup and calibration, and asset retirement obligations. Two conditions govern the decision — future economic benefits must be probable, and cost must be measurable — and capitalised cost can never exceed the benefits expected.

For assets a company builds for itself, capitalise direct materials and labour, a reasonable share of overhead, and interest on debt raised to finance the construction, during construction only.

After the asset is in service the test is whether the spending extends its life or usefulness beyond the original expectation. Improvements are capitalised; routine repairs and maintenance are expensed as incurred.

Depreciation

Depreciation allocates cost systematically to expense over the periods the asset helps produce revenue — moving cost from the balance sheet to the income statement. It is not a valuation exercise and it is not a cash flow. Two estimates are required: useful life (economic, not physical) and residual value.

Accumulated depreciation is a contra asset that grows over time; cost less accumulated depreciation is book value. A truck at 80,000 with 12,000 of accumulated depreciation is reported net at 68,000.

Changing an estimate

Estimates can be revised, and revisions apply prospectively — prior years are never restated. Extend the truck's life from five to seven years after four years of straight-line depreciation: book value is 80,000 − 57,600 = 22,400, remaining life is 5 − 4 + 2 = 3 years, so year 5 depreciation is (22,400 − 8,000) / 3 = 4,800.

Disposals and impairment

Sale proceeds above book value give a gain, below it a loss, reported within income from continuing operations but non-recurring in nature. Selling that truck for 25,000 against book value of 22,400 gives a 2,600 gain.

When an asset's recoverable amount falls materially below book value, an impairment loss is recognised immediately. Note the second-order effect: a write-down reduces future depreciation, so it flatters the margins of the years that follow.

One thing to remember
Because estimates are involved, liabilities are where profit gets quietly shifted between years. Under-estimate today, and today’s profit looks better.

Sources and uses

Assets are the uses of funds — the investments management has made. Liabilities and equity are the sources. Financial leverage rises whenever assets are funded with liabilities rather than equity.

Current liabilities fall due within a year and are often non-interest-bearing, so companies stretch them where they can. Long-term liabilities usually carry interest, and the sensible strategy is to match the life of the funding to the life of the asset it finances.

Trade payables and cash discounts

Payables arise from buying goods or services on credit. They are free financing: inventory arrives now and is paid for later, with no interest charge. Increasing payables improves operating cash flow and leaves profitability untouched, while reducing net working capital.

Credit terms often include a discount. "2/10, n/30" means 2% off if paid within ten days, otherwise the full amount in thirty. Under the net-of-discount method a 160 purchase is capitalised at 156.80. Pay late and the extra 3.20 is recorded as interest expense — discounts lost — rather than as more inventory. Annualise that 2% for twenty days and it is expensive money.

Accrued liabilities

Expenses incurred but not yet paid: wages, vacation pay, interest, taxes, rent, utilities, warranties. Each creates a liability and an expense simultaneously.

The lever
Because accruals are estimates, management can shift income between periods with them. Underestimating accruals understates liabilities and expenses, which overstates income and retained earnings. The shortfall does not disappear; it lands in a later period.

Provisions, warranties, contingencies

Not every obligation is certain. A provision is recognised when an outflow is probable — at least around a 50% chance — and the amount is reasonably estimable, measured at the most likely amount. Lawsuits, warranty claims and restructuring programmes are the usual cases.

Warranties show the mechanics cleanly. Estimate 2,000 of warranty cost at the time of sale: expense 2,000, liability 2,000, matched against the revenue that created the obligation. Later repairs of 400 draw the liability down to 1,600 and are not an expense of that later period.

A contingent liability — a potential obligation dependent on an uncertain future event, or a present obligation that is neither probable nor reliably measurable — is not recognised on the balance sheet. If material, it is disclosed in the notes.

Short-term debt

Typically a bank line of credit evidenced by an interest-bearing note, reported as a current liability. Borrowing itself has no effect on income or equity; interest accrues with time. Borrow 3,000 at 6% on 1 January and by 31 March you have accrued 3,000 × 6% × 3/12 = 45.

Two halves of equity

Contributed capital is the cumulative cash shareholders paid in for shares, less what has been paid back out to repurchase them. Earned capital is retained earnings — cumulative profits less dividends ever paid — plus accumulated other comprehensive income. If losses exceed cumulative profits, retained earnings becomes an accumulated deficit.

Counting shares

Authorised shares are the legal maximum. Issued shares are those actually sold. Outstanding shares are issued less treasury shares, and they are the ones that vote, receive dividends, and form the denominator of earnings per share.

Par value is an arbitrary legal amount per share with essentially no economic meaning. Issue 500 shares of 1 par at 15: cash 7,500, common stock at par 500, additional paid-in capital 7,000. Share issues never create revenue.

Preferred stock

Preferred shares carry priority over common — usually a fixed dividend paid first, and a preference in liquidation — generally without voting rights. Cumulative preferred means unpaid dividends accumulate and must be settled before any common dividend; arrears are disclosed rather than recognised until declared. A call feature lets the issuer repurchase at a set price. Economically, preferred sits between debt and equity.

Buybacks

A company buying its own shares reduces cash, equity and share count. Treasury transactions never produce a gain or loss in income: any difference between repurchase cost and reissue proceeds is recorded within equity. A company cannot report a profit from trading in itself.

They earn 200, so you add your 30% — 60 — to both your profit and the value of your stake. The last one, consolidation, surprises people: you report 100% of the subsidiary’s sales and costs, then show the part you do not own as “non-controlling interest”. So two companies with identical economics can show very different revenue, purely because one owns 51% and the other 49%.

One thing to remember
Fair value sounds objective, but it ranges from a real market price to a number out of a management model. The notes tell you which.

Buying other companies' securities

Firms hold financial investments to park surplus cash, to earn a return, or to build a strategic position. Debt investments — government and corporate bonds — are recorded at acquisition, accrue interest income, and are remeasured or held at amortised cost depending on classification.

For share investments, the accounting follows one question: how much influence does the investor have?

LevelTypical holdingTreatment PassiveUnder 20%Fair value, changes through profit or loss (or OCI where elected) Significant influence — an associateRoughly 20–50%Equity method Control — a subsidiaryOver 50%, or ability to elect the board majorityConsolidation, with non-controlling interest

The percentages are indicators, not the test. Contractual rights, board seats and shareholder structure can give control below 50% or deny it above 20%.

The equity method in one calculation

An associate carried at 1,000, a 30% holding. The associate earns 200 and pays 50 of dividends. Carrying amount becomes 1,000 + 60 − 15 = 1,045, and 60 flows into the investor's income. Dividends reduce the carrying amount rather than creating income, because they return value already recognised.

Consolidation changes how a group looks

Consolidation brings in 100% of a subsidiary's revenues, costs, assets and liabilities, then shows the share not owned as non-controlling interest within equity. An equity-method stake in the same business would appear as a single line on each statement. Identical economics, very different revenue, margins and asset base — worth remembering before comparing two groups with different structures.

Fair value, and where judgment enters

Fair value is what an independent buyer would pay. The hierarchy grades the evidence: Level 1 is a quoted price in an active market for an identical asset; Level 2 uses observable inputs for similar assets; Level 3 uses unobservable model inputs. Level 3 values are management's estimates, described as prices.

Earnings management
Whether fair value changes run through profit or through OCI is largely fixed at purchase, and that makes the classification decision itself consequential. Combined with discretion over when to realise gains, it gives management a quiet lever on reported income.

Why a third statement

Accrual profit and cash generation differ, sometimes for years. Cash pays wages, interest and dividends, so users need to see it directly: liquidity, meaning the ability to meet near-term obligations, and solvency, the ability to survive long term.

Cash equivalents — short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible to a known amount of cash with little risk of change in value — are added to cash and treated as one figure, because moving money between a current account and a treasury bill is not an economic event.

In plain words

You cannot compare a corner shop with a supermarket using raw numbers — one is a thousand times bigger. Turn everything into percentages and ratios and they become comparable. This chapter is the small set of ratios that does most of the work, and how to break them apart to see what drives them.

Start here

A worked set: profit 500, owners’ money 2,500, assets 5,000. ROE is 20%, ROA is 10%. The gap between them — 10 points — comes from borrowing. Debt lifts the owners’ return when things go well and deepens the hole when they do not.

Then one idea that explains most of what you will see. ROA breaks into two pieces you can multiply:

profit per sale × sales per unit of assets = return on assets

On sales of 10,000: profit margin is 5% and asset turnover is 2.0, and 5% × 2.0 = 10%. This is why a luxury brand and a supermarket can earn the same return by opposite routes — thick margins and slow turnover, or thin margins and fast turnover. Very few businesses get both.

One thing to remember
Ratios show symptoms, not causes. Two companies using different inventory or lease methods are not really comparable until you adjust for it.

Two ways to normalise

Raw figures cannot be compared across firms of different sizes or across years of different scale. Vertical, or common-size, analysis restates income statement items as a percentage of net sales and balance sheet items as a percentage of total assets. Horizontal analysis examines change across time. Neither requires a single ratio; both make patterns visible.

Start at the top: ROE

Return on equity is net income over average shareholders' equity — the return to the owners, leverage included. Net income of 500 on average equity of 2,500 is 20%.

Return on assets is net income over average total assets: the return on every unit invested, regardless of funding. 500 over 5,000 is 10%.

The difference between the two is the contribution of leverage, sometimes called return on financial leverage: 20% − 10% = 10 points. Debt raised at a cost below ROA lifts shareholder returns and magnifies losses with equal efficiency.

Break ROA into its two levers

ROA = Profit margin × Asset turnover = (Net income / Sales) × (Sales / Average assets)

On sales of 10,000: profit margin is 5%, asset turnover is 2.0, and 5% × 2.0 = 10% ROA. That identity explains why a luxury brand and a grocery chain can earn the same ROA by opposite routes — high margin and low turnover against thin margin and high turnover. Strategy usually decides which lever a firm pulls, and pulling both at once is rare.
